Digital Self-Defence

Your phone tracks you 800 times a day. Your data is
harvested, profiled, and sold. Scams targeting young people
have doubled. AI can clone your face and voice from thirty
seconds of video. This chapter is not about paranoia -- it is
about literacy. You are already being watched. The question
is whether you understand how, and whether you've done
anything about it.
The Scenario

     REAL STORY

 I t started with a text. "Hi Mum, I've lost my phone and I'm using
       a friend's number. Can you transfer $500 for rent? I'll explain
   later. Please don't call, I can't talk right now." The number was

   was at university in another city. It sounded like something she'd

   The scammer had scraped Liv's social media -- her name, her

This chapter is different from the others. The first three essentials --
silence, body, skills -- are about building internal foundations. This one
is about defending yourself in a world that is actively working against
you. The surveillance economy is not a conspiracy theory. It is a
business model. Your attention, your data, your behaviour, and your
identity are the product. Understanding this -- and learning to protect
yourself -- is now as essential as knowing how to cook or manage
your money.

Why This Essential Matters

You are the most surveilled generation in human history. Your phone
knows where you sleep, where you work, who you meet, what you
read, what you buy, what you search for at 2am, and how fast you
walk. It knows your heart rate, your sleep patterns, your menstrual
cycle, your political views, your sexual orientation, and your deepest
insecurities. It knows these things not because you told it, but because
you allowed it to collect them -- one permission, one app, one "agree
to all" at a time.

There are four reasons this matters:

1. Your data is not yours.

When you "agree to terms and conditions," you are giving away the
right to your own behaviour. Everything you do on a platform -- every
click, scroll, pause, and share -- is recorded, analysed, and sold. You
are not the customer. You are the inventory. The customer is the
advertiser who pays for access to your attention, shaped by a profile
so detailed it can predict what you'll do before you know it yourself.

2. Scams are now industrial-scale.

The "Hi Mum" text is the tip of the iceberg. Phishing emails, fake
investment schemes, romance scams, student loan fraud, parcel
delivery texts, the tax office refund scams -- these are not
opportunistic attempts by individuals. They are run by organised
criminal networks using AI to personalise attacks at scale. Scams
targeting 16­25 year olds have doubled since 2023. You are the target.

3. AI has changed the threat.

Two years ago, a deepfake took a team of VFX artists and a Hollywood
budget. Today, you can make one on your phone in five minutes. AI
can clone a voice from thirty seconds of audio. It can generate a video
of someone saying something they never said. It can write a text in
your mother's voice. The line between real and fake is dissolving --
and most people haven't noticed.
4. Privacy is a prerequisite for freedom.

Privacy is not about hiding bad things. It is about having the space to
think, question, doubt, and grow without being watched, judged, and
influenced at every turn. Surveillance changes behaviour. When you
know you're being watched, you conform. You self-censor. You
become what the algorithm expects you to be. Privacy is what allows
you to become who you actually are.

1. The Surveillance Economy

Let's understand what's actually happening when you use your phone.
The apps on your phone are not tools. They are intelligence-gathering
systems. Their purpose is not to serve you -- it is to extract data from
you that can be sold to the highest bidder.

How It Works

Every time you open an app, it collects data. Your location (GPS). Your
device ID. Your IP address. The time you spend on each screen. What
you tap. What you scroll past. What you hover over. What you almost
bought but didn't. This data is packaged, aggregated, and sold
through a chain of brokers you've never heard of, to advertisers you'll
never see, who use it to show you ads designed to exploit your
specific vulnerabilities at the moment you're most susceptible.

This is called "surveillance capitalism" -- a term coined by Harvard
professor Shoshana Zuboff. It is the dominant business model of the
internet. Your experience of the digital world is not the product. Your
behaviour is the product. The app is the bait; the data is the catch.

The Data Broker Chain

You give data to an app. The app gives it to a data broker. The broker
combines it with data from other apps, public records, and purchased
datasets. The result is a profile so detailed it knows your income, your
political leanings, your health conditions, your relationship status, your
travel patterns, and your psychological vulnerabilities. You never
consented to this profile. You never saw it. But it exists, and it's for
sale.

Government Surveillance

It's not just companies. Governments collect data too -- and they don't
always need a warrant. In the UK, the Investigatory Powers Act allows
security services to retain and search your browsing records. In
Australia, the Telecommunications (Interception and Access) Act
requires telecoms to store your metadata for two years. Metadata is
"data about data" -- who you called, when, for how long, where you
were -- and it paints a more complete picture than the content of the
call itself.

This is not about whether you trust your current government. It is
about whether you trust every future government. Data collected today
can be used in ways nobody has imagined yet. Once it exists, it cannot
be un-existed.
2. Your Digital Footprint

Your digital footprint is everything about you that exists online -- every
post, photo, comment, review, like, share, and tag, across every
platform, since the day you first touched the internet. Most of it you
forgot you made. All of it is still there.

The Permanent Record

The internet does not forget. That photo you posted at 14. That
comment you left on a YouTube video. That tweet you thought was
funny at 16. They are all still there -- cached on servers, archived by
the Wayback Machine, screenshotted by someone you'll never meet.
When you apply for a job in ten years, the first thing the recruiter will
do is Google you. What will they find?

     PRACTICAL TIPS -- AUDIT YOUR FOOTPRINT

    Google yourself. Use your full name in quotation marks: "Jane
       Smith." See what comes up on the first three pages.

    Check the Wayback Machine (archive.org). Enter your old
       social media URLs. See what's been archived.

    Search yourself on Pipl.com or similar people-search engines.
       See what data brokers have on you.

    Go through your old social media posts. Delete anything that
       doesn't represent who you are now.

    Set all your social media profiles to private. There is no reason
       for a stranger to see your posts.

The Right to Be Forgotten

Under GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) in the UK and EU,
you have the right to request that search engines remove links to
personal information about you that is "inadequate, irrelevant, or no
longer relevant." This is called the right to erasure. You can request it
directly from Google and Bing. It won't remove the original content --
but it will make it much harder to find. Use it.

What You Post vs What They Collect

There's a difference between what you voluntarily post and what is
collected without your knowledge. Your Instagram grid is your
voluntary footprint. Your location history, your search history, your app
usage patterns -- that's your involuntary footprint. You can clean up
the first one. The second one requires deeper intervention: deleting
accounts, using privacy-respecting alternatives, and refusing to use
services that don't respect your data.

3. Social Media Profiling

Algorithms don't just show you content. They study you. Every second
you spend on a post, every micro-pause before you scroll, every tap
and back-tap is recorded and fed into a model that predicts what will
keep you engaged. The model is extraordinarily good at its job. It
knows you better than your friends do -- because your friends don't
have 4,000 data points per day.

The Engagement Machine

The algorithm's goal is not to inform you, entertain you, or connect
you. Its goal is to keep you on the platform for as long as possible. It
does this by showing you content that triggers a response -- outrage,
amusement, validation, anxiety. The content that triggers the strongest
response is the content that gets shown to the most people. This is
why your feed gets darker, angrier, and more extreme over time. It is
not a coincidence. It is a design choice.

How Profiling Works

The platform assigns you to a "cohort" -- a group of people who
behave similarly. If you're in a cohort of 18­24 year-old males who like
gaming and follow certain political accounts, the algorithm will feed
you content that statistically keeps that cohort engaged. You are not
an individual to the algorithm. You are a data point in a cluster. Your
feed is shaped by the average behaviour of people like you -- and
your behaviour, in turn, shapes the average for everyone else.

The Manipulation You Can't See

Profiling isn't just about ads. It's about influence. If the algorithm
knows you're anxious, it can show you content that makes you more
anxious -- because anxious people spend more time scrolling. If it
knows you're lonely, it can show you content that makes you feel more
alone -- because lonely people don't close the app. The platform
profits from your pain. And it is very, very good at identifying it.
PRACTICAL TIPS -- LIMIT PROFILING

 Turn off "personalised ads" in your account settings on every
   platform. This stops them using your data to target ads.

 Clear your search and watch history regularly. Don't let the
   algorithm build a stable model of you.

 Use a privacy-respecting browser like Firefox or Brave. They
   block trackers by default.

 Install uBlock Origin to block ad and tracking scripts. It's free
   and takes two minutes.

 Use a non-Google search engine like DuckDuckGo for anything
   you don't want tracked.
4. AI Chatbots -- The Free Trap

If a product is free, you are the product. This has been true of social
media for years. It is now true of AI chatbots. When you type a
question into a free AI tool, you are not just getting an answer. You are
feeding the system. Every question reveals what you're thinking about.
Every prompt is a data point. The AI learns about you -- your interests,
your concerns, your plans, your vulnerabilities -- and that data is
valuable.

What You're Really Agreeing To

Most free AI chatbots reserve the right to store, analyse, and use your
conversations. Some use them to train future models. That means your
questions -- and the information you shared in them -- could end up in
the training data of a model that someone else queries later. If you
pasted a personal document, a work email, a medical question, or a
financial statement into a chatbot, that data may now be part of the
system. You can't un-ring that bell.

The Local Alternative

You don't have to use cloud-based AI. You can run AI models locally --
on your own machine, with no internet connection, no data leaving
your device. Tools like Ollama and LM Studio let you download open-

conversations stay on your computer. Nobody sees them. Nobody

PRACTICAL TIPS -- SAFER AI USE

   information into a free cloud AI chatbot.

 If you must use cloud AI, use paid tiers with explicit no-training
   policies -- and read the actual policy, not the headline.

 Treat AI chatbot data the way you'd treat a postcard: assume it
   can be read by anyone who handles it.
5. Malware, Scams & Ransomware

Let's get concrete. The threats are real, they're targeting you, and
they're getting better. Here's what to watch for and how to protect
yourself.

Phishing

Phishing is when someone pretends to be a legitimate organisation --
your bank, your university, the tax office, a delivery company -- to
trick you into giving them your password, card details, or personal
information. The emails and texts look real. The logos are correct. The
links seem genuine. But the domain is one letter off: "hsbc-
secure.com" instead of "hsbc.co.uk." The sender name says "Royal
Mail" but the email address is "no-reply@delivery-update-3847.com."

     PRACTICAL TIPS -- SPOTTING PHISHING

    Check the sender's actual email address, not just the display
       name. On a phone, tap the name to expand it.

    Hover over links before clicking. On mobile, long-press to
       preview the URL.

    Never enter your password after clicking a link. Go to the site
       directly by typing the URL.

    Legitimate organisations never ask for your full password, PIN,
       or one-time code by email or text.

    When in doubt, call the organisation directly using the number
       from their official website -- not the number in the email.

Malware and Ransomware

Malware is software designed to harm you. It can steal your data, log
your keystrokes, take over your camera, or encrypt your files and
demand payment to unlock them (ransomware). You get it by
downloading the wrong file, clicking the wrong link, or installing the
wrong app. Once it's in, it's very hard to get out.
     REAL STORY

 T om, 19, downloaded a "free" version of a paid productivity app

   malware that sat quietly on his laptop for three weeks, logging

Ransomware

Ransomware is the nastiest variant. It encrypts all your files and
displays a message: "Pay $500 in cryptocurrency within 48 hours or
your files are deleted permanently." It targets businesses, hospitals,
and universities -- but it also targets individuals. The payment doesn't
always work. The criminals may take the money and keep the files.
The FBI and NCSC both advise against paying. The best defence is
prevention.
PRACTICAL TIPS -- MALWARE DEFENCE

 Keep your operating system and apps updated. Updates patch
   security holes that malware exploits.

 Install antivirus software. Windows Defender is free and good
   enough for most people. Malwarebytes is a solid free
   complement.

 Never download "cracked" or "free" versions of paid software.
   They almost always contain malware.

 Don't plug in USB drives you find or are given. They can
   contain malware that auto-installs.

 Back up your important files to a cloud service AND an external
   hard drive. If you're hit by ransomware, you can wipe and
   restore.
6. Deepfakes & Synthetic Media

A deepfake is a video, image, or audio clip generated by AI that shows
someone doing or saying something they never did. The technology
has improved so fast that the best deepfakes are now indistinguishable
from reality to the human eye. This is not a future threat. It is
happening now.

Voice Cloning

AI can clone someone's voice from 30 seconds of audio -- which
means from any video they've posted online, any voice note, any
phone call. The clone can then say anything, in their voice, with their
accent and cadence. This is how the "Hi Mum" scam works at scale:
scrape the target's social media, clone their voice, call the parent, and
ask for money. It works because it sounds exactly like their child.

Video Deepfakes

Video deepfakes are harder to make but increasingly accessible. Apps
can swap faces in real time on video calls. A scammer can appear as
your boss on a Zoom call, ask you to transfer funds, and you won't see
the difference. This has already happened -- in 2024, a finance worker
at a multinational company transferred $25 million after a video call
with a deepfake CFO.

How to Spot a Deepfake

Deepfakes are getting better, but they're not perfect. Look for:

   Eye contact. Deepfakes often have slightly wrong eye movement
   or blink patterns.
   Mouth sync. Watch the lips. Minor mismatches between audio and
   movement are tell-tale signs.
Lighting and shadows. Light reflecting on the face that doesn't
match the environment.
Hands and fingers. AI still struggles with hands -- extra fingers,
wrong positions, morphing.
The gut test. If something feels off -- the tone, the request, the
urgency -- trust your instinct and verify through another channel.

PRACTICAL TIPS -- DEEPFAKE DEFENCE

 Establish a "safe word" with your family. If you get a distress
   call, ask for the word. No safe word = no trust.

 Never act on a financial request from a call, text, or video
   without verifying through a second channel you already trust.

 If a video call seems suspicious, ask the person to turn their
   head fully, hold up a hand, or move into different lighting.
   Deepfakes struggle with sudden, unpredictable movements.

 Be cautious about the amount of your face and voice you put
   online. Every clip is raw material for a clone.

7. Pattern of Life

In intelligence and military analysis, there is a concept called "pattern
of life." It refers to the behavioural fingerprint that analysts build from
tracking someone's movements, communications, and habits over
time. When you know someone's pattern of life, you can predict what
they'll do next. You can identify anomalies. You can find their
vulnerabilities.

The surveillance economy has automated this. Your phone builds a
pattern-of-life profile on you every day -- where you go, when you
sleep, who you're with, what you read, when you're most susceptible
to advertising. The algorithm knows your pattern of life better than you
do. And it uses that knowledge to predict and shape your behaviour.
Why Predictability Is Dangerous

If someone can predict your behaviour, they can manipulate it. If the
algorithm knows you check Instagram at 11pm every night, that's when
it shows you the most engaging content. If it knows you're most likely
to impulse-buy on a Sunday evening, that's when the ads appear. If a
scammer knows your routine -- when you're alone, when you're
stressed, when you're most likely to respond to a "Hi Mum" text --
they can time their attack for maximum effect.

Breaking the Pattern

One of the most powerful things you can do -- for your privacy, your
freedom, and your mental health -- is to break your patterns. Take a
different route to work. Leave your phone at home for an afternoon. Go
offline for a weekend. Change your routine enough that the algorithm
can't model you. You'll be harder to track, harder to manipulate, and --
perhaps more importantly -- you'll rediscover what it feels like to be
unpredictable. To be free.

This connects directly to Essential 1 (Silence). The practice of sitting in
silence is, among other things, an act of refusal -- a refusal to be
tracked, stimulated, and shaped for ten minutes. Every time you put
your phone down, you're breaking the pattern. You're becoming less
predictable. You're becoming more yourself.

      PAUSE AND REFLECT

    How predictable are you? If someone tracked your phone for a
    week, what would they learn? What time you wake up. What route
    you take. Who you text first. What you search for at night. Is that a

8. Mental Health Online

The attention economy is not just a privacy issue. It is a mental health
issue. The same systems that track you are designed to keep you
engaged -- and keeping you engaged often means keeping you
anxious, angry, or insecure. You cannot protect your mind from
surveillance without also protecting it from manipulation.

The Attention Economy

Your attention is the most valuable resource in the digital economy.
Every app, every platform, every service is competing for it. They have
invented increasingly sophisticated ways to capture and hold it --
infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications, streaks, red badge counts,
variable rewards. These are not features. They are hooks. And they are
designed by people who study human psychology for a living.

The Comparison Trap

Social media presents a curated version of reality: everyone's best
moments, best angles, best achievements, best holidays. Your brain
compares this highlight reel to your own unedited life -- and you lose
every time. This is not a character flaw. It is a design consequence.
The platform profits from your comparison. The worse you feel, the
more you scroll. The more you scroll, the more data they collect. The
more data they collect, the more they can sell.

Doomscrolling

Doomscrolling -- compulsively scrolling through negative news -- is
not a habit you chose. It is a behaviour the algorithm encourages
because negative content generates more engagement than positive
content. Outrage keeps you scrolling. Fear keeps you scrolling. Hope
does not. The algorithm has learned this, and it feeds you accordingly.
Your feed is not a reflection of the world. It is a reflection of what
keeps you on the platform.

9. Practical Digital Defence

Enough theory. Here is your toolkit -- the specific, concrete steps you
can take today to protect yourself. None of these require technical
knowledge. All of them are free or nearly free. Together, they will put
you ahead of 95% of the population.

1. Passwords

Use a password manager. Bitwarden is free, open-source, and
excellent. Generate a unique, random password for every account.
Never reuse passwords. If you use the same password for three
services and one gets breached, all three are compromised. A
password manager means you only need to remember one master
password -- the manager handles the rest.

     PASSWORD RULES

    Use a password manager (Bitwarden, 1Password, KeePassXC).
       Stop trying to remember passwords.

    Every account gets a unique, randomly generated password.
       No exceptions.

    Your master password should be a passphrase -- four random
       words strung together. Longer is stronger.

    Never write passwords on sticky notes, in Notes apps, or in
       text files.

    Check haveibeenpwned.com regularly. It tells you if your email
       or password has been in a known breach.

2. Two-Factor Authentication (2FA)

2FA means you need two things to log in: your password AND a code
from your phone. Even if someone steals your password, they can't
get in without the second factor. Turn it on for every account that
offers it -- especially email, banking, and social media. Use an
authenticator app (Google Authenticator, Authy, Aegis) rather than
SMS codes, which can be intercepted through SIM-swapping.

3. Encrypted Messaging

Use Signal for private messages. Signal is free, open-source, and end-
to-end encrypted -- meaning even Signal can't read your messages.
WhatsApp is also end-to-end encrypted, but it collects more metadata
and shares it with Meta. For anything sensitive -- health, relationships,
finance -- use Signal.

4. Browsing

Switch to Firefox or Brave as your default browser. Both block trackers
by default. Install uBlock Origin to block ads and tracking scripts. Use
DuckDuckGo instead of Google for searches you don't want tracked.
Use a VPN (ProtonVPN free tier is good) on public Wi-Fi to prevent
network-level tracking.

5. Phone Privacy

Go through your phone's app permissions. Most apps don't need your
location, camera, microphone, or contacts. Revoke everything that
isn't essential. Turn off "advertising identifier" in your phone's privacy
settings. Turn off location history in Google Maps. Use an ad-blocker
on your phone's browser.
PHONE PRIVACY CHECKLIST

   Permission manager. Revoke what isn't needed.

 Turn off "Significant Locations" / "Location History." Your
   phone has been mapping your life. Delete the history.

10. Building Resilience

Tools and settings will protect you today. Habits will protect you for
life. The digital landscape will change -- new threats, new platforms,
new scams. The specific defences in this chapter will become
outdated. The principles will not.

The Habits That Last

   Assume nothing is private by default. Treat every online
   interaction as if it could be read by a stranger. Because it could be.
   Verify before you trust. A message from a "friend" in distress. A
   "bank" asking for your details. A "colleague" requesting a transfer.
   Verify through a second channel. Every time.
   Minimise your surface area. The fewer accounts, apps, and
   services you use, the less of you exists to be exploited. Delete
   accounts you don't use. Uninstall apps you don't need.
   Update everything. Software updates patch security holes.
   Ignoring them is like leaving your front door unlocked. Turn on
   automatic updates.
   Back up your data. A cloud backup and an external hard drive
   backup. If you're hit by ransomware, a thief, or a crashed device,
   your data survives.
   Keep learning. The threats will change. Follow reputable sources:
   the NCSC (National Cyber Security Centre), Electronic Frontier
   Foundation (EFF), and The Verge's privacy section. Stay informed.

   Silence (Chapter 1): The practice of silence is the foundation of
   digital self-defence. You cannot recognise manipulation if you
   never step away from it long enough to see it. Silence is where
   clarity lives -- and clarity is what makes you harder to fool.
Body (Chapter 2): Sleep deprivation and poor diet make you more
susceptible to manipulation and scams. Your brain on no sleep is
not a brain that makes good security decisions. Protect your body
to protect your mind.
Skills (Chapter 3): Digital self-defence is itself a real-world skill --
as practical as cooking or budgeting. Understanding how to secure
your accounts is as essential as knowing how to read your payslip.
Community (Chapter 5): Talk about digital threats with your friends
and family. Share what you've learned. A community that
understands digital self-defence is harder to scam, harder to
manipulate, and harder to divide.
Knowledge (Chapter 6): Understanding how surveillance,
algorithms, and AI work is knowledge without Wi-Fi. When you
understand the system, you can defend against it -- even when the
technology changes. Running local AI models is itself an act of
digital self-defence.
Nature (Chapter 7): Time in nature breaks the digital pattern. It
removes you from the surveillance ecosystem and reminds you
that the world is larger than a screen. Nature is the ultimate offline
space.
Convictions (Chapter 8): Privacy is a value worth defending. The
conviction that you have the right to think, question, and grow
without being watched -- that is a conviction worth holding.
Without it, the other essentials are compromised by the very
systems that track you.
REAL STORY

I didn't think about privacy at all until someone used my photos
   to make a fake account. They took pictures from my Instagram
-- which was public, because I never thought about it -- and
created a profile with my name, my face, and my details. They
started messaging my friends, asking for money. Two of them
almost paid. I only found out because a friend texted me: "Why are
you asking me for $200 on Instagram?"

I went private on everything that day. I deleted old posts. I checked
what was out there. I was horrified. Years of my life, just sitting
there for anyone to use. Now I check my privacy settings every
month. I use Signal for anything private. I don't post my face on
public accounts. I'm not paranoid -- I'm just aware. I wish I'd been
aware sooner.

-- Jess, age 21

"Do This This Week" Challenges

     CHOOSE YOUR CHALLENGE

    Level 1: The Privacy Audit

   For one hour this week, audit your digital life. Google yourself.
   Check your social media privacy settings. Review your phone's
   app permissions. Check haveibeenpwned.com. Write down three
   things you found that surprised you -- and fix them.

    Level 2: The Password Overhaul

   Install a password manager (Bitwarden is free). Change your
   passwords for your three most important accounts -- email,
   banking, and phone provider. Turn on 2FA for each. Use unique,
   randomly generated passwords. This will take 30 minutes. It will
   protect you for years.

    Level 3: The Digital Sabbath

   For one full day this week -- 24 hours -- no social media, no news
   apps, no streaming. Phone calls and messages from real people
   only. Notice what happens to your mood, your sleep, and your
   anxiety. Notice how often you reach for your phone and what that
   tells you.

    Level 4: The Deeper Clean

   Delete one social media account you don't need. Revoke all
   unnecessary app permissions on your phone. Switch your default
   browser to Firefox or Brave. Install uBlock Origin. Set up Signal and
   invite three people you're close to. This is the start of a more
   private digital life.
